SCIENCE COMMUNICATION CHANNELS AND PUBLIC TRUST: A 68-COUNTRY ANALYSIS
Group 7 — MAKINDE, MOYINOLUWA, NWACHUKWU, OLAKUNLE, OLORUNFUNMILAYO, OLORUNTOBI, OLUWARANTI


ABSTRACT

Public trust in science has become increasingly fragmented across national
contexts, shaped in part by the channels through which citizens encounter
scientific information. Using the Trust in Science and Science-Related
Populism (TISP) dataset — a cross-sectional survey of 71,922 respondents
across 68 countries collected between November 2022 and August 2023 — this
study models the pathways linking science communication channel use to
public trust in scientists. We apply a multi-level regression framework to
partition within- and between-country variance in trust, structural
equation modelling to test whether science-related knowledge mediates the
relationship between communication frequency and trust, and comparative
analyses across high-income and low/middle-income country groupings.

The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.105 indicates that
10.5% of variance in trust is attributable to country-level factors,
confirming meaningful cross-national heterogeneity. Structural equation
modelling revealed a positive indirect effect of communication frequency
on trust operating through science knowledge (path a = 0.139, path b =
0.325, indirect effect = 0.045), with a smaller but significant direct
effect (c' = 0.070). A one-way ANOVA found significant differences in
mean trust by primary communication channel (F = 27.963, p < 0.001), with
newspaper and television users reporting the highest trust and family/friends-
and social-media-primary users reporting the lowest. These findings suggest that science
communication interventions targeting channel diversity and science
literacy simultaneously are most likely to improve public trust, and that
country-level structural factors warrant explicit modelling in any global
survey of trust in science.


INTRODUCTION

Trust in science underpins the public uptake of evidence-based policy on
issues ranging from vaccination to climate mitigation, yet that trust is
neither uniform nor stable across the globe. A growing body of research
links declining or polarised trust in science to the fragmentation of
media environments: as audiences move from traditional gatekept outlets
(newspapers, television, peer-reviewed journals) toward algorithmically
curated social platforms, the correlation between communication channel
choice and epistemic outcomes has drawn increasing scholarly attention
(Reif et al., 2025). This fragmentation is not merely an aesthetic
concern for media scholars; it carries tangible consequences for public
health and democratic governance. When citizens encounter scientific
information primarily through informal channels — social media, messaging
apps, or word of mouth — they are exposed to a different ecology of
claims, framings, and credibility cues than those who rely on edited
newspaper science journalism or peer-reviewed literature. Whether and how
this difference translates into differential trust in scientists is an
empirical question that has received surprisingly limited attention at
global scale.

Theorists of science communication have proposed at least two distinct
mechanisms through which channel choice might affect trust. The first is
an epistemic or knowledge-building mechanism: certain channels — those
associated with higher editorial quality, scientific accuracy, and depth
of coverage — may increase science knowledge, which in turn raises trust
(Allum et al., 2008). If this mechanism dominates, then the channel
matters mainly because of what it teaches. The second mechanism is a
credibility or source-cue mechanism: people may infer the credibility of
science itself from the credibility of the channel through which they
encounter it, independently of the actual information content. Television
or newspaper science journalism carries implicit institutional endorsement;
social media posts carry the authority of the poster's social network,
which may or may not include scientists. Both mechanisms predict a
channel-trust association, but they have different implications for
intervention: the first implies that improving content quality is
sufficient; the second implies that channel choice itself needs to be
addressed.

Existing cross-national work has typically examined trust in science as a
function of political ideology, religiosity, or general institutional
trust (Huber et al., 2019, as cited in Reif et al., 2025), while
comparatively few studies model communication channel use as a mediating
mechanism at global scale (Loglisci & Liguori, 2021). Single-country
studies from the United States and Western Europe have found that social-
media reliance is negatively associated with trust in science (Reif et
al., 2025), but it remains unclear whether these findings generalise to
countries with very different media infrastructures, literacy rates, and
cultural orientations toward expertise. A study that surveys 68 countries
and models the communication-knowledge-trust pathway explicitly is
therefore a meaningful contribution beyond the existing literature.

The TISP Many Labs collaboration (Mede, Cologna, et al., 2025) closes
part of this gap by fielding a harmonised, 37-language questionnaire
across 68 countries, enabling — for the first time at this scale — a
joint test of channel use, science knowledge, and trust within a single,
comparably measured dataset. TISP's careful pre-registration, expert
translation, and local data-collection partnerships make it the most
comprehensive cross-national science trust survey conducted to date.

This study asks: How do science communication channels and demographic
factors influence public trust in science across cultures? We test a
mediation pathway in which the frequency of science communication exposure
predicts self-reported science knowledge, which in turn predicts trust in
scientists, while accounting for country-level clustering through a
multi-level modelling framework. We further compare high-income and
low/middle-income country groupings, motivated by evidence that the
communication ecologies and institutional trust baselines of these groups
differ systematically (OECD, 2024). We hypothesise (H1) that higher
communication frequency will positively predict trust; (H2) that science
knowledge will partially mediate this relationship; and (H3) that there
will be significant between-country variance in trust scores after
controlling for individual-level communication and knowledge variables.
Confirming all three hypotheses would suggest that both channel-level
and country-level interventions are warranted; disconfirming H3 would
suggest that individual-level communication and knowledge factors are
sufficient to explain cross-national differences without invoking country
cultural or institutional context.


METHODS

Data Acquisition

Data come from the TISP (Trust in Science and Science-Related Populism)
dataset (Mede, Cologna, Berger, et al., 2025), a pre-registered,
cross-sectional online survey of N = 71,922 participants across k = 68
countries and territories, fielded November 2022 to August 2023 by the
TISP Many Labs consortium. The cleaned dataset (ds_main.csv) was
downloaded directly from the project's Open Science Framework repository
(osf.io/5c3qd), which is licensed CC BY 4.0. The survey measured trust in
science and scientists, science-related populist attitudes, perceptions of
science's societal role, science communication behaviour, climate
attitudes, personality traits, political/religious orientation, and
demographics. The dataset received ethics exemption from the Area
Committee on the Use of Human Subjects at Harvard University (August
2022).

The final analytic sample comprised 71,883 respondents (39 cases excluded
by listwise deletion on at least one of: overall trust, communication
frequency, or science knowledge; exclusion rate < 0.1%). Group sizes per
country ranged from 325 to 8,127 respondents (mean = 1,057).

Harmonisation Protocol

All analyses used the real TISP column names from ds_main.csv (semicolon-
delimited, UTF-8 with BOM). Trust in scientists was operationalised as the
row mean of 12 Likert items (TRUST_SCI_expert through TRUST_SCI_otherviews,
scale 1-5), providing a composite overall trust score. Science knowledge
was proxied by TRUST_METHOD (trust in the scientific method, 1-5 Likert),
following common practice in cross-national science literacy research
(Allum et al., 2008). Communication frequency was the row mean of 10
SCIINFO_ channel-frequency items (SCIINFO_newspapersmags through
SCIINFO_rlconversations, 1-5 scale). Primary communication channel was
determined by selecting, for each respondent, the SCIINFO_ column with the
highest score and mapping it to one of seven canonical labels (Social
Media, Television, Newspapers, Online News Sites, Scientific Journals,
Family/Friends, Other). Country codes are ISO-3 as provided in
COUNTRY_CODE; World Bank 2022-23 income classifications were applied via a
hardcoded lookup table (see data/metadata/data_dictionary.csv). Full
variable definitions and proposed roles are documented in
data/metadata/data_dictionary.csv (47 key variables annotated).

XSD Design Rationale

To satisfy the assignment's XML/XSD modelling requirement and to provide a
portable, self-describing representation of the survey structure
independent of any single statistical package, we modelled the dataset as
an XSD schema (xml/tisp_schema.xsd) with a root Respondent element nested
under Dataset/Metadata and Dataset/Respondents. Each Respondent element
carries Demographics, Trust_Scale, Populism, Communication, Climate, and
PoliticalReligious child elements, mirroring the eight construct domains
documented in the original TISP questionnaire (Mede, Cologna, et al.,
2025). Likert-type items are constrained to a decimal range of 1-5 via a
shared LikertScoreType restriction, and categorical fields (e.g., primary
communication channel, country income group) are constrained via
enumerations, so that schema validation itself functions as a
data-quality check. A 3,000-respondent XML instance (xml/tisp_full.xml)
was generated from the real data using code/csv_to_xml.py and validated
against the schema using lxml's XMLSchema validator (result: VALID = True,
zero schema errors).

XPath/XQuery Specifications

Two XPath expressions and three XQuery scripts were developed against the
schema (full listing and outputs in xml/xpath_xquery_scripts.md). The
primary XPath expression,
  //tisp:Respondent[tisp:Trust_Scale/tisp:overall < 3
                    and tisp:Communication/tisp:primary = 'Social Media'],
isolates low-trust respondents whose dominant information source is
social media, operationalising the assignment's specified filter. The
primary XQuery script computes, for each country, the mean trust score
and the modal (dominant) communication channel, providing the per-country
descriptive layer that underlies the choropleth visualisation in Results.
All XPath and XQuery scripts operate on the tisp: namespace prefix
(http://group7.tisp.org/schema) and are designed for execution in Saxon
or BaseX.

Analytic Strategy

Four analyses were specified in the assignment brief and implemented in
code/analysis.py:
(1) A multi-level (mixed-effects) linear regression of trust on
    communication frequency and science knowledge, with a random
    intercept for country, estimated by REML via statsmodels MixedLM.
    The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was computed as
    var_country / (var_country + var_residual).
(2) A structural equation model (semopy) testing the mediation pathway
    communication frequency -> science knowledge -> trust, decomposed
    into direct effect c', indirect effect a*b, and total effect c'+a*b.
(3) A choropleth map (plotly) of mean trust score by country, using ISO-3
    codes and a Red-Yellow-Blue colour scale (saved to
    figures/trust_choropleth.html).
(4) A one-way ANOVA (scipy.stats.f_oneway) testing whether mean trust
    differs across the seven primary communication channel groups, with a
    grouped boxplot saved to figures/anova_channel_boxplot.png.
All analyses were conducted in Python (pandas >= 2.0, statsmodels >= 0.14,
semopy >= 2.3, plotly >= 5.20, scipy >= 1.10); code and a documented
column-mapping step are provided in the GitHub repository for full
reproducibility.

Somewhat counter-intuitively, lower-middle-income country respondents
reported the highest mean trust (M = 3.746, SD = 0.747), while upper-
middle-income countries showed the lowest mean trust (M = 3.599, SD =
0.736). High-income and low-income countries showed nearly identical mean
trust (M = 3.615, SD = 0.676 and M = 3.613, SD = 0.755, respectively) —
a gap of 0.002 scale points that is substantively negligible. The pattern
confirms a non-linear relationship between country wealth and public trust
in science: the middle of the income distribution is not the middle of the
trust distribution. The standard deviations are broadly similar across
income groups (range 0.676 to 0.755), indicating comparable within-group
heterogeneity everywhere rather than greater polarisation in any one
economic stratum. Median trust values (50th percentile) are consistently
slightly above means, reflecting mild left skew in the distribution; most
respondents express moderate-to-high trust in scientists, with the left
tail driven by a minority of strongly distrustful respondents. The
primary channel distribution shows that newspapers (N = 21,497, 29.9%)
and television (N = 17,585, 24.5%) together account for over half of
respondents by primary channel, while scientific journals are the least
common primary source (N = 1,415, 2.0%), as would be expected given
the specialist nature of academic literature.

Figure 1 -- Choropleth of mean trust in scientists by country
(figures/trust_choropleth.html; Red-Yellow-Blue colour scale, range 3.15-4.24)

The choropleth map reveals notable geographic clustering. South Asia
(India, M = 4.24) and sub-Saharan Africa (Nigeria, M = 3.95; Ghana,
M = 3.88) cluster toward the highest trust end of the spectrum.
English-speaking Oceania (Australia, M = 3.94; New Zealand, M = 3.93)
and Spain (M = 3.89) are among the highest-trust high-income countries.
Lowest-trust countries are concentrated in Eastern Europe and Central Asia
(Kazakhstan, M = 3.15; Albania, M = 3.16; Russia, M = 3.23) and Japan
(M = 3.37), consistent with the East Asian pattern of reserved public
deference to authority coexisting with high scientific output.

ICC (country-level variance share): 0.105

The multi-level model converged successfully (REML). The ICC of 0.105
indicates that 10.5% of total trust variance is explained by country-
level factors after controlling for individual communication frequency
and science knowledge. Both fixed-effect predictors are highly significant:
each one-unit increase in communication frequency is associated with a
0.067-point increase in trust (p < 0.001), and each one-unit increase in
science knowledge with a 0.317-point increase in trust (p < 0.001). The
knowledge effect is approximately 4.7 times the size of the communication
frequency effect (0.317 vs. 0.067), a disparity that has direct implications
for intervention design: if practitioners aim to improve trust, efforts
that build genuine science knowledge will likely yield a greater return
than efforts that simply increase exposure frequency. The country-level
random-effects variance of 0.040 (Group Var) is small but non-negligible
relative to the residual variance of 0.341, yielding the ICC of 0.105.
This indicates that country context explains meaningful additional variance
in trust beyond what individual-level predictors can account for, which
justifies the multi-level specification and is consistent with prior
cross-national evidence on the role of institutional trust and media
freedom as country-level moderators of science trust (Wellcome Trust, 2020).

The one-way ANOVA is highly significant (F = 27.963, p < 0.001). Newspaper
users report the highest mean trust (M = 3.665), followed by television
users (M = 3.652). Social-media-primary and family/friends-primary
respondents report the lowest trust (M = 3.579 and 3.565 respectively).
Differences between groups are modest but robust given the large sample.
The boxplot (figures/anova_channel_boxplot.png) shows broadly overlapping
distributions across all groups, centred around 3.5-3.7.

The ordering of channel groups by mean trust is consistent with a
credibility-hierarchy model: channels with higher editorial gatekeeping
and institutional affiliation (newspapers, television) sit at the top;
informal, peer-network channels (family/friends, social media) sit at the
bottom; and intermediate channels (online news, scientific journals) fall
in between. The fact that Scientific Journals users (M = 3.597) do not
rank highest is noteworthy — they represent a small, likely highly
educated self-selected group, and ceiling effects on trust may be absent
because scientifically engaged respondents may also hold scientists to a
higher critical standard. Taken together, the four analyses provide a
consistent and internally coherent picture: more and better science
communication predicts more trust, but the country you live in and the
channel you primarily use both modulate that baseline.


DISCUSSION

The four analyses presented here converge on a consistent interpretation:
the relationship between science communication and public trust is real,
globally generalised, and operating substantially through epistemic
mechanisms — that is, through science knowledge as a mediator — rather
through pure exposure effects alone. At the same time, both country-level
context and primary channel choice exert additional and independent effects
that an individual-level analysis would miss. We discuss each finding in
turn before addressing limitations and policy implications.

The ICC of 0.105 confirms that approximately one tenth of the variance in
trust in scientists is explained by country membership, a non-trivial
proportion that justifies multi-level modelling as a design requirement
for cross-national trust research. Country-level factors — which include
institutional quality, scientific capacity, political culture, and
historical legacies of expert authority — are distinct sources of
variation that cannot be captured by individual-level survey items alone.
This finding aligns with prior work using the Wellcome Global Monitor
showing similar levels of between-country clustering in trust in scientists
(Wellcome Trust, 2020), and it has a direct implication for practice:
interventions designed to raise public trust must account for the
contextual baseline of their target country. A global or regional science
communication campaign that ignores country context is likely to be
inefficient: the same message may substantially shift trust in one country
while having negligible effect in another, depending on prior institutional
trust, media infrastructure, and cultural orientation toward expert
authority.

The SEM mediation result is the most theoretically significant finding of
this study. The indirect effect of communication frequency on trust,
operating through science knowledge (a*b = 0.045), is positive and
statistically significant, indicating that the mechanism through which
science communication raises trust is, at least partially, an epistemic
one: more frequent exposure to scientific information is associated with
higher trust in the scientific method, which is in turn associated with
stronger trust in scientists. The direct effect (c' = 0.070) is also
positive and significant, suggesting that mere exposure to science
communication — beyond its effect on knowledge — carries a positive
association with trust. This is consistent with familiarity and parasocial
contact theories of attitude formation (Cacioppo & Petty, 1989), in which
repeated exposure to a source type can itself generate positive affect
independent of information processing. Importantly, however, knowledge
is a substantially stronger predictor than communication frequency alone
(b = 0.325 vs. direct effect c' = 0.070), suggesting that the quality of
engagement with scientific information matters more than quantity. Science
communicators should therefore prioritise content and format strategies
that build genuine understanding rather than maximising reach alone.

The ANOVA result — that primary channel significantly predicts trust
(F = 27.963, p < 0.001), with newspaper and television users reporting
higher trust than social-media and family/friends users — is consistent
with a channel credibility model: institutionalised, gatekept channels
(traditional media with professional editorial standards) are associated
with modestly higher trust in scientists compared with informal or
algorithmically curated channels. However, the differences in means are
small (range approximately 0.10 of a scale point across channels), and the
overlapping boxplot distributions caution against treating channel as a
strong individual-level determinant of trust. Selection effects are almost
certainly at play: people who already trust scientists may be more likely
to read newspapers or science-oriented content, producing a correlation
without a causal channel-to-trust direction. Randomised exposure studies
at national scale would be required to disentangle this.

The geographic pattern in the choropleth is substantively interesting.
India, sub-Saharan African countries (Nigeria, Ghana), and Australia/New
Zealand cluster toward the high-trust end, while Eastern European and
Central Asian countries (Kazakhstan, Albania, Russia) and Japan cluster
toward the low-trust end. The high-trust pattern in lower-middle-income
countries such as India and Nigeria, which also appear in Table 1's income-
group comparison (lower_middle_income mean = 3.746), may reflect lower
baseline scepticism toward expert institutions in developmental contexts
where science is more strongly associated with economic progress and public
health improvement. Japan's low trust relative to its scientific
infrastructure and high-income status is consistent with prior cross-
cultural findings linking collectivist deference and institutional
restraint to a measured (rather than sceptical) expression of trust
on surveys (Nakayachi et al., 2020). These geographic patterns confirm
that culture and institutional history cannot be reduced to economic
development as predictors of public trust in science.

Limitations: TISP trust and knowledge measures are self-report, collected
via a single cross-sectional wave, so causal directionality between
communication use, knowledge, and trust cannot be established — reverse
causation (higher-trust individuals selectively engaging with certain
channels) remains plausible. The 37-language survey instrument, while
extensively validated by the TISP consortium, may not achieve full
measurement invariance across all 68 cultural contexts (Mede, Cologna, et
al., 2025), which is a general caveat for any single-model cross-national
comparison of this kind. Additionally, the multi-level model used a random-
intercept specification only; random slopes for communication frequency and
knowledge across countries were not estimated, which may understate
country-level heterogeneity in the predictor-outcome relationships.
TRUST_METHOD (trust in the scientific method) was used as a science
knowledge proxy in the absence of a direct knowledge quiz in this wave of
TISP; it measures epistemic orientation rather than factual recall, which
may overestimate the knowledge-trust overlap.


CONCLUSION

Using a 71,922-respondent, 68-country dataset and a multi-method analytic
approach, this study provides evidence that science communication channels
and science knowledge jointly predict public trust in scientists at global
scale. Communication frequency has a positive, partially mediated effect
on trust (total effect = 0.115 on a 1-5 scale; indirect through knowledge
= 0.045); approximately 10.5% of trust variance is attributable to country-
level contextual factors; and newspaper/television primary users report
modestly but significantly higher trust than social-media and family/
friends primary users. On the basis of these findings, we recommend that
science communication policy prioritise dual interventions: expanding the
volume of trustworthy science journalism in traditional and digital media,
while simultaneously investing in science literacy education to ensure
that communication exposure translates into genuine knowledge gains rather
than passive familiarity alone. Future research should follow up with
longitudinal or experimental designs to establish the causal direction of
these relationships across diverse cultural contexts.
