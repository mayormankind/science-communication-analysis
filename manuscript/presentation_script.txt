SLIDE 2 — RESEARCH QUESTION AND MOTIVATION
=============================================================================

The question at the heart of this project is a timely one: How do science
communication channels and demographic factors influence public trust in
science across cultures?

We live in a world where the same scientific consensus on vaccines, climate,
or nutrition can be received very differently in Lagos, Tokyo, or Berlin. A
large part of that difference, we argue, comes from where people get their
science information. Someone who reads scientific journals may have a very
different relationship with scientists than someone who relies primarily on
Facebook or WhatsApp. And yet almost no study had tested this idea at truly
global scale — across dozens of countries, dozens of languages, with a
harmonised questionnaire — until the TISP dataset came along.

We hypothesised, first, that greater communication frequency would predict
higher trust; second, that science knowledge would mediate that relationship;
and third, that there would be meaningful variation in trust between countries
even after controlling for individual factors. These three hypotheses drove
all four of our analyses.

=============================================================================
SLIDE 3 — DATASET
=============================================================================

Our data come from the TISP dataset — the Trust in Science and Science-
Related Populism survey — published in Scientific Data in 2025 by Mede,
Cologna, and a consortium of 100 researchers. The survey reached 71,922
respondents in 68 countries and territories, fielded between November 2022
and August 2023, in 37 languages. It is publicly available from the Open
Science Framework at osf.io/5c3qd under a CC BY 4.0 licence, and the
ethical exemption was granted by the Harvard University Area Committee on
the Use of Human Subjects.

The key variables we used were: trust in scientists, measured as the mean
of 12 Likert items on a one-to-five scale; science knowledge, proxied by
trust in the scientific method, again one to five; communication frequency,
as the mean of ten channel-frequency questions asking how often respondents
get science information from newspapers, TV, social media, books, and so on;
and primary communication channel, derived by identifying which of those ten
SCIINFO columns had the highest score for each respondent. We also used
country income group from World Bank classifications, and the country code
for multi-level grouping. Our full data dictionary, documenting 47 key
variables, is at data/metadata/data_dictionary.csv in the repository.

After removing 39 cases with missing data on our key variables — less than
0.1 percent — our analytic sample was 71,883 respondents in 68 countries.

SLIDE 4 — XML / XSD MODELLING (DEMO SECTION)
=============================================================================

Now let me turn to the XML modelling component, which is a core requirement
of this assessment.

We designed an XML schema — tisp_schema.xsd — that captures the full
structure of the TISP survey. The schema has a root element, TISP_Dataset,
containing Metadata and a Respondents collection. Each Respondent element
has six child elements: Demographics, Trust_Scale, Populism, Communication,
Climate, and PoliticalReligious. This mirrors the major construct domains
of the original TISP questionnaire.

To ensure data quality, we applied XSD constraints directly in the schema.
Likert scores are typed as decimal values between one and five using a shared
LikertScoreType restriction. Primary communication channel is constrained to
a fixed enumeration: Social Media, Television, Newspapers, Online News Sites,
Scientific Journals, Family/Friends, and Other. Country income group is also
enumerated. This means that if you tried to load a respondent with a trust
score of six, or a channel value of "YouTube," the schema validator would
immediately flag it.

[PRESENTER: If you have a BaseX screenshot showing this — refer to it here]

We generated a 3,000-respondent XML file from the real data using our
csv_to_xml.py script and validated it against the schema using Python's lxml
library. The validation printed: VALID: True — zero schema errors.

For XPath, we implemented the expression specified in the assessment outline:
  //tisp:Respondent where Trust_Scale/overall is less than 3
  and Communication/primary equals Social Media
This extracts low-trust respondents whose primary science information source
is social media — a combination that our results confirm is associated with
the lowest trust scores in the dataset.

For XQuery, we implemented the required per-country summary query, which for
each of the 68 countries returns the mean trust score and the dominant
communication channel. This feeds directly into the choropleth visualisation
you'll see in a moment. All XPath and XQuery scripts are documented in
xml/xpath_xquery_scripts.md.

SLIDE 6 — KEY RESULTS: MULTI-LEVEL MODEL
=============================================================================

For our first inferential analysis, we fitted a multi-level regression of
trust on communication frequency and science knowledge, with a random
intercept for country. The model converged successfully with 71,883
respondents and 68 countries.

The intraclass correlation coefficient — our key output — was 0.105. This
means that 10.5 percent of the total variance in trust is explained by
country-level factors, confirming that where you live matters, independently
of your individual communication habits and science knowledge. This justifies
our use of multi-level modelling: treating all 71,000 respondents as if they
were independent would have been a serious methodological error.

At the individual level, science knowledge was the stronger predictor:
each one-unit increase on the knowledge scale was associated with a 0.317-
point increase in trust, controlling for communication frequency, p less than
0.001. Communication frequency also had a significant positive association
with trust: beta equals 0.067, p less than 0.001.

=============================================================================
SLIDE 7 — KEY RESULTS: SEM MEDIATION
=============================================================================

Our second analysis was a structural equation model testing the mediation
pathway from communication frequency through science knowledge to trust.

The results clearly support partial mediation. Path a — communication
frequency to knowledge — had a coefficient of 0.139, p less than 0.001:
more frequent science exposure is associated with higher trust in the
scientific method. Path b — knowledge to trust — was 0.325, p less than
0.001: higher epistemic orientation is strongly associated with trusting
scientists. The indirect effect, a times b, was 0.045: this is the portion
of the communication-trust relationship that operates through the knowledge
pathway. The direct effect, c-prime, was 0.070, also significant, giving a
total effect of 0.115.

What this tells us is that communication works on trust in two ways: by
building knowledge — the epistemic route — and by a smaller direct exposure
effect, possibly through familiarity and parasocial contact. But knowledge
is clearly the more powerful lever.

=============================================================================
SLIDE 8 — KEY RESULTS: ANOVA
=============================================================================

Our fourth analysis was a one-way ANOVA testing whether mean trust differs
across the seven primary communication channel groups.

The result was highly significant: F equals 27.963, p is 1.46 times ten to
the minus 33 — so essentially zero. Newspaper users had the highest mean
trust at 3.665, followed by television at 3.652. Social media users had a
mean of 3.579 and family/friends users 3.565 — the lowest two groups.

The differences are statistically robust but practically modest: about 0.10
of a scale point separates the top from the bottom. The boxplots show
broadly overlapping distributions, which cautions against treating channel
as the primary driver of trust at the individual level. Selection effects
are almost certainly present: people who already trust science may gravitate
toward newspapers and away from social media.

=============================================================================
SLIDE 9 — CONCLUSION AND RECOMMENDATIONS
=============================================================================

So what do we conclude?

Science communication channels and science knowledge jointly and
significantly predict public trust in scientists across 68 countries. Around
10.5 percent of trust variance is explained at the country level, confirming
that culture and institutions matter beyond individual-level communication
behaviour. The knowledge mediation pathway is real and substantial: the most
direct route to raising trust through communication is one that genuinely
builds understanding, not one that simply maximises reach.

Our practical recommendation for science communicators and policymakers is a
dual intervention: invest in quality science journalism through traditional
and digital channels — channels associated with higher trust — while
simultaneously supporting science literacy education, so that communication
exposure translates into genuine knowledge gains rather than passive
familiarity.

Future research should use longitudinal or experimental designs to establish
causal direction, and should test whether the communication-knowledge-trust
pathway holds equally across all 68 countries or whether there are important
cross-national moderators.

Thank you. We're happy to take questions.
